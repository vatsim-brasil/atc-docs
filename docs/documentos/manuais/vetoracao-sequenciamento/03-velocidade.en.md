# Speed Control

## Why control speed

Speed is the cheapest sequencing tool. It adjusts spacing without lengthening the track, without taking the aircraft off its procedure and with almost no workload for the pilot. ICA 100-37 presents it in exactly those terms: speed control is used "in order to facilitate sequencing or to reduce the need for vectoring"[^1].

Speed control has two limits. It acts **slowly**: it takes time and distance to have an effect, especially at high levels and with the aircraft clean[^2]. And it **costs fuel** if kept for a long time[^3]. That is why it works best early, while the aircraft is still far away.

## General rules

| Rule | Source |
| --- | --- |
| The speed control remains in force **until explicitly cancelled or amended** by the controller. | [^4] |
| Cancelling the speed control does not release the aircraft from the **speed limits of the airspace class**, such as 250 kt IAS below FL 100. | [^4] |
| Speed control is **not** applied to aircraft entering or established in a holding pattern. | [^5] |
| Speed control should be limited to what is **necessary** to establish or maintain separation or spacing. Avoid frequent changes, especially alternating increases and reductions. | [^6] |
| If the pilot is unable to comply, the pilot says so, and **you apply another method**. | [^7] |
| The pilot keeps the speed within **±10 kt** or **±0.02 Mach**. | [^8] |
| Express speed in **multiples of 10 kt IAS**. At or above FL 250, you may use multiples of **0.01 Mach**. | [^9] |
| Tell the aircraft when the speed control is **no longer required**. | [^10] |

The speed limits for each airspace class are in the [Airspace and ATS Services Manual](../espaco-aereo-servicos-ats/02-classes.en.md#tabela-consolidada-anexo-ii-da-ica-100-37).

## Limits on arrival

An arriving aircraft may be instructed to maintain its **maximum speed**, **minimum clean speed**, **minimum speed** or a specified speed[^11]. "Minimum clean speed" is the lowest speed possible without flaps, speed brakes or landing gear.

| Phase | Limit | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial descent from cruising level, turbojet | Below **250 kt** IAS only with the crew's agreement | [^12] |
| Below FL 150, turbojet | Reductions down to **220 kt** IAS, which is usually the minimum clean speed | [^13] |
| Intermediate and final approach phases | Only small changes, of up to **±20 kt** IAS | [^14] |
| From **4 NM from the threshold** | **No** speed control | [^15] |

Let the aircraft operate clean for as long as possible[^13]. The crew normally stabilizes the approach (speed and configuration) at about 3 NM from the threshold[^15], so speed instructions must end before that.

!!! warning "Descending and slowing down do not mix"
    Avoid asking at the same time for a **high rate of descent and a speed reduction**. The aircraft is normally unable to do both, and a significant reduction during descent may require it to level off temporarily[^16]. For the same reason, avoid applying horizontal and vertical speed control **at the same time** to the same flight[^17].

Speed restrictions published on SIDs and STARs remain in force unless you explicitly cancel or amend them[^18]. Some of them exist because of RNAV navigation requirements, such as the maximum speed on a radius-to-fix (RF) leg.

## Method

### Who to adjust first

To open the spacing between successive aircraft, **slow down the one behind first, or speed up the one ahead**. Then adjust the others, in order[^19]. Slowing down the one ahead first compresses the whole line.

### IAS, TAS and compression during descent

The instructed speed is the **indicated** airspeed (IAS). What separates aircraft is the **true** airspeed (TAS) or, more precisely, the ground speed. At a constant IAS, TAS **decreases** as the aircraft descends[^20].

The consequence: two aircraft descending at the same IAS, with the one ahead lower, **close in on each other**, because the one ahead has a lower TAS. To keep the distance, the one ahead must fly a higher IAS. A rule of thumb is **6 kt IAS for every 1,000 ft of difference** in height. Below FL 080, the difference between IAS and TAS is negligible for this calculation[^20].

!!! example "Example"
    Aircraft A is descending through 6,000 ft. Aircraft B, right behind, is passing 11,000 ft. That is a 5,000 ft difference, or about 30 kt. For the distance between them not to decrease, A must fly about 30 kt IAS faster than B. For example, A at 250 kt and B at 220 kt.

### Reference speeds

!!! note "Good practice"
    Standardized speeds simplify the sequence: if all aircraft on the same segment fly the same speed, the spacing between them is maintained. A common scale for jets is:

    | Position | Speed |
    | --- | --- |
    | Initial descent and long downwind | 250 kt |
    | Short downwind and base | 210 to 220 kt |
    | Intercept vector | 180 kt |
    | Final, until 4 NM from the threshold | 160 kt |

    Turboprops and light aircraft fly slower. Confirm that the requested speed is possible for the type, especially on final.

## Vertical speed control

The controller may also adjust the **rate of climb or descent**, to establish or maintain vertical separation between two climbing or two descending aircraft[^21]. The rules follow the same logic as horizontal speed control: only what is necessary, without frequent changes, with notice when the restriction no longer applies, and with an alternative method ready in case the aircraft is unable to comply[^22].

The aircraft may be instructed to maintain a specific rate, a rate equal to or greater than a value, or a rate equal to or less than a value[^23]. Before applying it, check **down to which level** the aircraft can maintain the requested rate[^24].

> **PT MMH, perform rate of descent 1,500 feet per minute.**
>
> **TAM 3932, maximum rate of descent 2,000 feet per minute.**
